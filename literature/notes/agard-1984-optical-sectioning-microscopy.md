## Why This Matters

This is the classic early review of optical sectioning: a through-focus series of 2-D images as the raw material for 3-D recovery, the 3-D PSF as the object-to-image blur, and the first generation of computational (deconvolution-type) methods with their practical limits. It supports the thesis statement that software solutions need the PSF and are computationally demanding, but it is a 1984 account of wide-field 3-D sectioning, not of aberration correction.

## Relevance to the thesis

- Supports: G30 (a through-focus series of images as the basis of 3-D recovery, pp. 191, 193, 198-199). G25 in part: computational recovery needs knowledge of the PSF (p. 204), is difficult and computationally expensive for large fine-spaced stacks (pp. 201, 204, 212, 216), and the early methods rely on empirical scaling constants and are sensitive to them (pp. 206, 209), ill-conditioning and ripples (p. 210).
- Does not support: G24 (aberration sources are not discussed; the PSF is idealized and computed, p. 196); noise amplification as an explicit statement (only "noise ... compounds" the negative-value problem, p. 212); "artifact-prone" in the sense of the thesis (it names ripples and contrast over-emphasis, pp. 210, 212-213); G26 and G27; any claim that cost is a problem today (the 1984 run times are tied to VAX hardware and the author expects costs to fall, p. 219); anything about light-field or Fourier light-field microscopy.

## Differences to state if cited

- 1984 review of wide-field optical sectioning of 3-D specimens with computed ideal PSFs; the thesis concerns a 2-D fluorescent scene imaged with a Fourier light-field microscope and a non-deconvolution method.
- Cost and timing statements refer to 1984 hardware and should not be quoted as current figures.
- The methods tested were evaluated on simulated test images in this paper.

## Safe Wording

Early computational optical-sectioning methods recover the in-focus object by reversing the 3-D convolution of the object with the microscope PSF, which requires knowing that PSF and was described as extremely difficult and computationally very expensive for large stacks with fine plane spacing (Agard 1984, p. 204).
