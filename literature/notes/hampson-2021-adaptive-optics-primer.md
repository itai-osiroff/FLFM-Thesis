## Why This Matters

A recent multi-field primer that states, in one place, that microscopy aberrations come from the optics and from refractive-index structure of the sample, that both direct (Shack-Hartmann) and indirect (sensorless) sensing are used in microscopy, which correctors are used, and that the isoplanatic patch can be as small as a few microns. It is a good citable source for the hardware-AO sentence of Chapter 1/2, but it also contains the caveats (indirect sensing preferred in microscopy; computational AO treated only briefly).

## Key Claims (paraphrased)

- **Sample-induced aberration in microscopy (Introduction, PDF p. 2).** Microscopes give blurred images when the sample has a non-uniform refractive index. [anchor: "microscopes produce blurred images"]
- **Sources of aberration: optics vs sample (Results/Microscopy, PDF p. 20).** Two origins are separated. System aberration comes from the microscope components, does not change in time, and is seen as a distorted PSF from a small fluorescent bead. Sample aberration arises from refractive-index mismatch between the sample and the immersion medium; it can severely degrade imaging, especially at high resolution. [anchor: "mismatches of the sample refractive index from that of the immersion"]
- **Super-resolution is more sensitive (PDF p. 20).** Super-resolution methods are even more sensitive to aberrations than diffraction-limited ones; samples a few microns thick can substantially reduce image quality. [anchor: "is even more sensitive to optical aberrations than the diffraction-"]
- **Aberrations in Table 1 (Table 1, PDF p. 53).** Main source in microscopy is the shape and refractive-index inhomogeneity of cells and tissue; magnitudes vary with sample and are mostly temporally static; peak-to-valley from submicrometre to several microns. [anchor: "Main source is shape and refractive index"]
- **Spatial and temporal behaviour (Aberration characteristics, PDF p. 8).** No comprehensive statistical model exists for microscopy; flat homogeneous samples are dominated by Zernike orders up to the fourth, complex samples need many more modes. Sample aberrations in live specimens usually change slowly, so measurement and correction need not be fast. [anchor: "there are no comprehensive statistical models"] [anchor: "do not vary rapidly over time"]
- **Aberrations vary spatially and with depth (Generic AO system, PDF p. 5; Results/Microscopy, PDF p. 20).** In microscopy there can be a volume of inhomogeneous tissue in front of the object, so the wavefront depends on where the object lies; sample aberrations "may vary spatially" and sometimes evolve over time and must be measured in situ. [anchor: "a volume of inhomogeneous tissue in front of the object of interest"] [anchor: "Sample aberrations may vary spatially and sometimes evolve over time"]
- **Definition of direct vs indirect sensing (Aberration measurement, PDF p. 8).** Direct sensing uses a dedicated wavefront sensor; indirect sensing infers the aberration from images and is called sensorless AO. Indirect methods need many images and take seconds to minutes versus milliseconds for a sensor. [anchor: "indirect methods can take seconds to minutes to determine"]
- **Why microscopy uses indirect sensing (PDF p. 8).** Because microscopy aberrations are mostly static, indirect methods suit this field; for astronomy, where turbulence changes fast, the slow speed would be a significant problem. [anchor: "aberrations in microscopy are mostly static, indirect methods"]
- **Sensorless AO confined to microscopy (Generic AO system, PDF p. 5).** Using image quality to drive the corrector reduces system complexity but is slow and unsuitable for rapidly changing aberrations, so the technique is mainly confined to microscopy. [anchor: "this technique is mainly confined to microscopy"]
- **Both sensing types are used in microscopy (Results/Microscopy, PDF p. 20; Table 1, PDF p. 53).** The text says both direct and indirect sensing are used. Direct (Shack-Hartmann) applies to transparent samples or to shallow depths of opaque ones; indirect works with no or substantial scattering. Table 1 lists microscopy sensors as "indirect sensing and SH are both common". [anchor: "Both direct and indirect wavefront sensing methods are used"] [anchor: "SH are both common"]
- **Shack-Hartmann sensor (Direct sensing, PDF p. 9).** The most widely used direct sensor in all fields; lenslet array plus camera in a pupil-conjugate plane; each spot shifts with the local wavefront slope. Traditionally it needs light from a point source (for microscopy, e.g. a fluorescent bead); spot elongation from axially extended objects is worse at higher NA, so it occurs more in microscopy than in vision science. [anchor: "most widely used sensor to measure the aberrations across all"] [anchor: "traditional implementation of the SH sensor requires light returning from"]
- **Image-based SH (PDF p. 9).** Some astronomy systems use an extended scene imaged behind each lenslet, with the shift of each image giving the local slope; this is starting to be adopted in microscopy. [anchor: "forms behind each lenslet, and the shift of each of these images"] [anchor: "This technique is starting to be adopted"]
- **Zonal indirect sensing uses image shifts (Indirect sensing, PDF p. 11).** In pupil-segmentation schemes, the required tilt of each pupil zone is determined from shifts in the image, so that rays meet at the focus; piston is then set from intensity measurements. [anchor: "determined from shifts in the image by ensuring rays meet"]
- **Tip and tilt equal image shift (Aberration characteristics, PDF p. 7).** Tip and tilt represent a shift of the image position and are usually not corrected in microscopy with stationary samples. [anchor: "Tip and tilt represent a shift in the position of the image"]
- **Phase retrieval with defocus (Indirect sensing, PDF p. 11).** Simplest phase retrieval uses two images of a fluorescent bead with different known defocus; commonly used in microscopy for static system aberrations (relevant to G30, but bead-based, not extended scenes). [anchor: "but known values of defocus and is commonly used in microscopy"]
- **Correctors (Aberration correctors, PDF p. 11).** Three types: deformable mirrors, liquid-crystal SLMs, deformable phase plates. Continuous-surface DMs are the most common across fields. [anchor: "are the most commonly employed corrector across fields"]
- **DM vs SLM trade-offs (PDF pp. 12-13; Microscopy Results, PDF p. 20).** LCSLMs need quasi-monochromatic linearly polarized light, so a DM is more suitable for multi-wavelength fluorescence; DMs are generally faster than LCSLMs and phase plates; LCSLMs have many pixels and are used on laser illumination. Phase plates currently have far fewer actuators. [anchor: "monochromatic polarized light because the corrector affects wavelengths differently"] [anchor: "generally faster than LCSLMs and deformable phase plates"]
- **Stroke and corrector selection (PDF pp. 12-13).** Maximum correctable peak-to-valley wavefront is twice the stroke for a DM. Cost, size, stability and linearity are named as secondary selection factors, with no figures given. [anchor: "is twice the stroke, as the additional path length"] [anchor: "such as cost, physical size, transmissivity/reflectivity, stability"]
- **Typical microscopy hardware (Table 1, PDF p. 53).** Continuous DMs most widely used, typically under 100 actuators; SLMs on laser paths, typically 512 x 512 pixels; segmented DMs for high-order scattering compensation; microscopy AO is open- or closed-loop, with loop dynamics set by imaging speed. [anchor: "Typical number of DM"]
- **Cost and complexity (PDF pp. 6, 13, 27, 28).** Multiconjugate AO adds significant cost and complexity (PDF p. 6). Cost is also listed among secondary corrector-selection factors (PDF p. 13). Ophthalmic AO carries high cost and complexity (PDF p. 27, vision section, not microscopy). Widespread adoption in biological imaging relies on systems that are more compact, less expensive and easier to use; integrating reflective correctors into an existing microscope is not trivial because of the extra conjugate plane and incidence angle (Outlook, PDF p. 28). The text gives no cost figures and does not say that DMs themselves are expensive. [anchor: "This adds significant cost"] [anchor: "relies on making systems more compact, less expensive and easier to use"] [anchor: "Integrating these reflective devices into an existing system, bespoke"]
- **AO is not yet widely adopted commercially (PDF p. 19; PDF p. 24).** AO has not yet been widely adopted commercially in microscopy; applications remain mostly proof-of-principle. [anchor: "yet been widely adopted in the commercial sphere"] [anchor: "applications of AO to microscopy remain mostly proof-of-principle"]
- **Isoplanatic patch definition and size (Generic AO system, PDF p. 6).** The isoplanatic patch is the area over which the aberrations can be treated as invariant. In microscopy it ranges from hundreds of microns (mouse brain) to a few microns in tissue with high curvature or complexity (zebrafish larvae, C. elegans). [anchor: "area over which the aberrations can be considered invariant"] [anchor: "In the case of microscopy, the patch size ranges from"]
- **Single corrector as default, hence one correction per field (PDF p. 6).** A single corrector (single-conjugate AO) is the most widely used implementation; multiple correctors (MCAO) add cost and complexity; a corrector conjugate to the dominant aberrating layer (sample-conjugate AO) can widen the sharp field. The text does not literally say "one correction over the whole field"; that is an inference from the isoplanatic-patch definition. [anchor: "Use of a single corrector is referred to as single-conjugate AO"] [anchor: "ground-layer AO in astronomy and sample-conjugate AO in"]
- **Spatially varying correction by tiling (Results/Microscopy, PDF p. 21; Limitations/Microscopy, PDF p. 28).** In lattice light sheet microscopy of zebrafish embryos, fast direct sensing measured excitation and detection aberrations in 140 local volumes, and these localized corrections were needed for diffraction-limited resolution throughout, because high curvature gives small isoplanatic patches. The Limitations section says direct sensing is fast enough to allow region-specific corrections throughout 3D volumes. [anchor: "wavefront sensing measured the excitation and detection aberrations in 140 local"] [anchor: "localized corrections were required to achieve diffraction-limited resolution"] [anchor: "allows region-specific AO corrections to be"]
- **MCAO unproven for continuous aberrations (PDF p. 28).** MCAO has enlarged the patch only in samples with a dominant aberrating layer; for samples with aberration throughout the volume it is open whether MCAO helps, and the authors call for correctors with pixels distributed in 3D. [anchor: "it remains to be seen whether MCAO leads to much"]
- **Computational AO and deconvolution (Outlook, PDF p. 29).** Computational AO is not new: it was proposed over 20 years ago, modelling aberrations by ray tracing through a mapped refractive-index distribution then deconvolving. The refractive index map is not practically obtainable for most systems; it has been shown for OCT with post-processing correction; phase diversity can also remove aberrations computationally in fluorescence images. The authors prefer physical correction over deconvolution because the extra corrected measurement yields more photons, increases SNR substantially and validates the correction. [anchor: "computational AO was proposed more than 20 years ago"] [anchor: "refractive index distribution is not practically possible for most systems"] [anchor: "Physical correction is preferred over deconvolution"] [anchor: "substantially increasing the signal to noise ratio"]
- **Machine learning for sensorless AO (PDF p. 29).** Expected to reduce the number of measurements needed by indirect methods, but not yet ready for complex specimens; needs large training data and is sensitive to system-specific conditions. [anchor: "machine learning will help to minimize the number of measurements"]
- **Scattering is out of scope (PDF p. 28).** The AO methods in the primer correct aberrations, not scattering; wavefront shaping addresses scattering in opaque samples. [anchor: "aim to correct aberrations but not scattering"]

## Relevance to the thesis

- Supports: G24 (aberrations arise from optics and from the sample refractive index; severe for high-resolution and super-resolution imaging; PDF p. 20 and Table 1, PDF p. 53). G26 (hardware AO = wavefront sensor, typically Shack-Hartmann, plus corrector; the paper also states that sensorless AO is the method suited to microscopy, PDF p. 8, and that both are used, PDF p. 20). G27 (isoplanatic patch size, small in complex tissue; tiling into local corrections; MCAO adds cost and complexity; adoption needs cheaper, simpler systems; correctors differ in speed, polarization and chromatic behaviour, PDF pp. 6, 12-13, 21, 28). G25 only weakly (computational AO and deconvolution mentioned in one paragraph, PDF p. 29).
- Does not support: G25 claims that software solutions are slow, artifact-prone, or amplify noise; the paper does not say any of these. It says only that physical correction is preferred because it collects extra photons. It does not say deformable mirrors are expensive (no cost figures for DMs or SLMs) and does not state a high-NA-specific aberration statement for fluorescence widefield imaging (high NA appears only for SH spot elongation, PDF p. 9, and laser processing). It says nothing on light-field microscopy, digital refocusing or G30 beyond bead-based phase retrieval with two defocus images. It does not support the claim that direct wavefront sensing is the standard in microscopy.

## Differences to state if cited

- In microscopy this primer reports indirect (sensorless) AO as the common and better-suited route because aberrations are static; a sentence that equates microscopy hardware correction with "wavefront sensing plus AO" should say direct sensing is one of two options (see also Ji 2010 caveat).
- Direct SH sensing in microscopy needs a point-like reference (bead or guide star) or an image-based variant (PDF p. 9); the paper says nothing about FLFM, which uses an extended 2D scene and image shifts between views.
- The primer's DM/SLM statements are qualitative; any cost wording in the thesis must not be attributed here as a number.
- Its spatial-variation statement is about isoplanatic patch and local volumes in 3D samples, not about a 2D scene with aberrations modelled as shifts.
- The paper treats AO as aberration correction only, not scattering inversion (PDF p. 28), consistent with the thesis scope.

## Safe Wording

Adaptive optics measures the aberrated wavefront, either directly with a wavefront sensor such as a Shack-Hartmann sensor or indirectly from images, and applies the opposite phase with a corrector such as a deformable mirror or spatial light modulator (Hampson et al. 2021, "Aberration measurement" and "Aberration correctors", PDF pp. 8-13). In microscopy the indirect, image-based route is common because sample aberrations are mostly static, and the area over which a single correction holds, the isoplanatic patch, can shrink to a few microns in complex tissue (PDF pp. 8, 6).
