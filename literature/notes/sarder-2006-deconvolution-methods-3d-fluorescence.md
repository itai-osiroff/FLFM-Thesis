## Why This Matters

A signal-processing overview of computational deconvolution for 3-D fluorescence microscopy (widefield and confocal). It gives a citable primary-source statement of the image-formation model (PSF convolution plus noise), why direct inversion is ill-posed and noise-amplifying, the families of algorithms, blind PSF estimation, and the computational burden. It is a good fit for G25, and its conclusion that the shift-invariant PSF assumption is not true in practice marks where spatially varying aberrations break standard deconvolution.

- **Two blurs, optical vs computational (p. 33, Deconvolution in fluorescence microscopy).** Fluorescence systems blur through both the microscope PSF and random noise. A shift-variant PSF can be assumed in (3), but in general the PSF is taken as shift-invariant for deconvolution. The Poisson model is called physically correct for photon-limited recording, and the additive Gaussian model is a simplification that works at high SNR. [anchor: "production of negligibly small extrinsic noise"] [anchor: "model is physically correct because the photon-limited image"]

## Relevance to the thesis

- Supports: G25. PSF knowledge or estimation is central (p. 34-35, 39-40); direct inversion amplifies noise (p. 37); linear methods give ringing artifacts and are sensitive to PSF errors (p. 37-38); iterative statistical and blind methods are computationally intensive and slow to converge (p. 38-39, 44-45); blind deconvolution can diverge and is non-unique (p. 40, 43). Table 2 (p. 44) summarizes this per method. Weakly G24: real-setup PSFs depart from design and show phase aberration (p. 34-35). Weakly G30: the 3-D data are a focal-plane series and deconvolution acts on that stack (p. 34).
- Does not support: G26 and G27. A search of the text finds no wavefront sensing, Shack-Hartmann, adaptive optics or deformable mirrors, and no statements on their cost or spatial invariance. It does not say that high NA specifically worsens aberrations, and it does not use the terms "depth-variant" or "spatially varying aberration" (it says "shift-variant" once, and that shift-invariance "is not true in practice"). It treats widefield and confocal z-stacks, not light-field or 2-D scenes. It does not say deconvolution is expensive in money terms. Do not cite it for the claim that deconvolution cannot correct sample-induced or spatially varying aberrations; it only says the shift-invariant assumption fails in practice.

## Differences to state if cited

- It treats deconvolution as a 3-D (z-stack) problem for widefield and confocal microscopy; the thesis uses a single 2-D light-field capture and no deconvolution.
- The paper does not frame deconvolution as the software alternative to adaptive optics; that framing is the thesis's own.
- The "slow" and "costly" statements concern iterative and statistical methods (and the authors' own parametric ML runs), not all computational correction. Simple linear filters are described as simple and fast, though noise-amplifying (Table 2, p. 44).
- "Needs PSF estimation" applies to non-blind methods; blind methods estimate the PSF jointly, at added cost and non-uniqueness.
- It is a 2006 overview and does not cover learned or more recent methods.

## Safe Wording

Computational correction relies on deconvolution, which needs a known or estimated PSF; direct inversion amplifies noise, linear filters produce ringing artifacts and are sensitive to PSF errors, and the iterative statistical and blind methods are computationally intensive (Sarder and Nehorai 2006, pp. 37-39, 44-45). Most such algorithms also assume a shift-invariant PSF, which the authors state does not hold in practice (p. 44).
