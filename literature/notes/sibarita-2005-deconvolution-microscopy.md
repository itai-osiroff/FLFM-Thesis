## Why This Matters

A broad review of 3D wide-field deconvolution microscopy. It states plainly that deconvolution needs a PSF (theoretical, measured or blind-estimated), that noise and PSF errors cause artifacts, that iterative statistical methods are slow, and that refractive-index mismatch and lens imperfections produce spherical aberration that breaks the shift-invariant PSF assumption. It is a good primary source for the thesis claim about software solutions (G25) and for the shift-variance caveat (G24, G27 spatial variation), but it is about 3D optical-sectioning wide-field/confocal stacks, not light-field microscopy.

## Key Claims (paraphrased)

- **Deconvolution is a computational restoration step (Sec. 1, p. 203).** Deconvolution is presented as a mathematical post-processing alternative to confocal pinholes; it is described as computationally heavy, yet runnable on inexpensive computers in anywhere from milliseconds to hours depending on stack size and algorithm. [anchor: "can now be processed by inexpensive computers, in a time period"]
- **Noise limits deconvolution (Sec. 2, p. 205).** Photon (Poisson) noise dominates and strongly limits the efficiency of deconvolution algorithms; it must be handled inside the algorithm or by filtering, and low signal-to-noise ratio is typical for living-cell work. [anchor: "considerably limits the efficiency of deconvolution algorithms"]
- **The PSF must be known (Sec. 2.4, p. 210).** The PSF describes the microscope response and is required by most deconvolution algorithms. Noise, wrong aberration estimates or wrong PSF scaling can cause major artifacts in the restored image. [anchor: "This information is required for most deconvolution algorithms"] [anchor: "incorrect estimates of aberrations and incorrect"]
- **Theoretical versus measured PSF (Sec. 2.4, pp. 210-211).** A theoretical PSF is noise-free and avoids a time-consuming bead measurement, but theory covers only perfect lenses and calibrated light paths. Real lens irregularities and some spherical aberrations cannot be predicted, and any mismatch between the theoretical and actual PSF lowers deconvolution efficiency and can create artifacts. A measured PSF from sub-resolution beads, taken under the same conditions as the sample, is advised. [anchor: "theoretically estimated PSF and the actual PSF of a microscope"] [anchor: "it is advisable to measure"]
- **Neither theory nor measurement gives the PSF inside a real sample (Sec. 2.4, p. 214).** The PSF changes with emission wavelength, axial distance from the coverslip and refractive indices (p. 212). Passing through a heterogeneous medium of unknown refractive index changes the PSF in a way neither theory nor bead experiments can predict. The text says it is generally not possible to inject beads into the specimen. Modelling a local PSF from DIC-derived refractive-index estimates with ray tracing is described, but then the PSF is no longer shift-invariant and the computation becomes extremely long (Fig. 9). [anchor: "experimental methods can predict the change in PSF caused by the passage"] [anchor: "to be shift-invariant, limiting deconvolution due to the extremely large"]
- **Aberrations break shift invariance (Sec. 2.2, p. 208; Sec. 2.4, p. 215).** Classical theory assumes a linear, shift-invariant PSF. In practice the PSF depends on where the point source is, most often axially. Astigmatism, coma and field curvature are described as negligible in good objectives, so the main concern is spherical aberration. [anchor: "shift invariance is not always demonstrated, and PSF"] [anchor: "are negligible and well corrected in high-quality objectives"]
- **Cause of the spherical aberration (Sec. 2.2, p. 208-209).** Light passing through layers with different refractive indices (living cell, closer to water at n=1.33 than to immersion oil at n=1.516) departs from the optical path the objective was designed for. Any deviation of coverslip, oil or sample parameters from design values causes spherical aberration (Gibson-Lanni model). Effects: asymmetric PSF, more flare, lower peak intensity, lower resolution and brightness in wide-field and confocal imaging, growing with depth. [anchor: "refractive index of a living cell culture is closer to that"] [anchor: "decrease in its maximal intensity. They decrease both resolution and brightness"] [anchor: "optimal values for which the optics were designed will cause spherical"]
- **Sources of aberration summarised (Sec. 2.4, p. 215; p. 211).** Refractive-index variation along the optical path and lens imperfections are named as the main causes of spherical aberration; the theory-versus-practice gap is said to be larger for high-NA oil-immersion objectives. Oil mismatch, objective defects, vibration and temperature-dependent oil properties are listed as measurable problems (p. 211). - **Ill-posed inversion and noise amplification (Sec. 4, p. 223; Sec. 4.1.3, pp. 224-225).** Restoration is an ill-posed problem with no single solution. Direct division by the OTF fails because the OTF falls to near zero outside its support, and noise is amplified, producing major artifacts. [anchor: "to the ill-posed problem category and does not generate a single solution"] [anchor: "noise is amplified during division"]
- **No best algorithm (Sec. 4.1.3, p. 225).** Each family works well for images with known properties and poorly otherwise; the best on simulated data is not necessarily best on real data. [anchor: "No best method has yet been identified in practical terms"]
- **Wiener inverse filter (Sec. 4.2, p. 226).** Direct, simple and cheap, but sensitive to the PSF; small PSF estimation errors can give major artifacts. Iterative methods need more computing power but are less sensitive to PSF error. The noise-to-signal constant K is unknown in practice. [anchor: "However, it is sensitive to PSF, and small errors in the"] [anchor: "approaches presented below, although more demanding in terms of computa-"]
- **Nearest-neighbour method (Sec. 4.3, p. 227).** Uses only the two adjacent planes; very rapid (two 2D convolutions per plane) but limited because other out-of-focus contributions are ignored. [anchor: "advantage of being very rapid"]
- **Constrained iterative (Jansson Van-Cittert, Gold) (Secs. 4.4-4.4.2, pp. 227-228).** Converge slowly and give poor results on noisy data, because unmodelled noise creates artifactual signal; noise control (Gaussian or Wiener smoothing of the first guess and of each of four to seven iterations) helps but costs sharpness. [anchor: "suffer from slow convergence of the solution"]
- **Maximum likelihood and Richardson-Lucy (Sec. 4.5.1, p. 230).** Richardson-Lucy is the p=1 case of the Poisson maximum-likelihood iteration. Described as the most frequently used for noisy data and robust to noise, but very slow (50 to 1000 iterations) and costly per iteration (two convolutions); without regularization noise can produce artifacts, so the first estimate is smoothed or iteration stopped early to prevent noise amplification. [anchor: "very slow convergence (between 50 and 1000 iterations)"] [anchor: "amplification during iteration"]

## Relevance to the thesis

- Supports: G25 (software solutions need a PSF and deconvolution): PSF requirement and its sensitivity (p. 210, p. 226); noise amplification in direct inversion (pp. 224-225) and in iterative schemes (p. 230); artifact risk from PSF errors and noise (p. 210, pp. 234-238); slow convergence of constrained iterative and MLE/Richardson-Lucy (pp. 228, 230) and the iteration counts; heavy computation (p. 203, pp. 239-242). Supports G24: aberrations from the sample (refractive-index heterogeneity, p. 214), its environment (media layers, oil and temperature, pp. 208-211) and the optics (lens imperfections, p. 215), worse for high-NA oil-immersion objectives (p. 208), and spherical aberration reducing resolution and brightness (p. 208). Partly supports G27 (spatial variation): the PSF is shift-variant in practice, but deconvolution assumes shift invariance and a spatially variant PSF makes computation very costly (p. 214); this concerns deconvolution, not deformable-mirror correctors. G30: weak background only (bead z-stacks for the PSF, p. 211-214).
- Does not support: G26 and G27 hardware claims (adaptive optics, wavefront sensing, Shack-Hartmann, deformable mirrors): not stated anywhere in the chapter. Nothing on light-field or Fourier light-field microscopy, sub-aperture views, view alignment, or lateral/local image shifts as an aberration model. No mention of "ringing" by name (only generic artifacts). Not a source for the claim that deconvolution is "costly" in money terms (see below). Does not describe any method as the only route for aberration compensation.

## Differences to state if cited

- The chapter treats 3D optical-sectioning stacks (wide-field and confocal) with a convolutional PSF model, not a single 2D light-field capture. Statements about cost and slowness refer to 3D stacks and iterative algorithms, with numbers from 2004-era PCs (PIV 2 GHz).
- It says deconvolution is "heavy" computationally but accessible on inexpensive computers (p. 203) and that Wiener filtering and nearest-neighbour methods are fast or modest in cost (pp. 226-227). So "slow and costly" is true of statistical and blind iterative methods (50-1000 iterations, blind about twice MLE time per iteration) but not of all software methods. The comparison of speeds is deliberately avoided in the Results section (p. 234).
- Noise amplification is stated for direct inverse filtering (pp. 224-225) and for unregularized iterative schemes (p. 230); the text also says statistical and noise-controlled algorithms are robust to noise (p. 237), and that Poisson MLE is robust to noise (p. 230). Cite precisely which algorithm class.
- The author's main aberration is axial spherical aberration from refractive-index mismatch with depth; other aberrations (astigmatism, coma, field curvature) are called negligible in good objectives (p. 208). Do not cite it for lateral, field-dependent aberrations of the kind the thesis models as view shifts.
- Blind deconvolution is described as estimating the PSF, but with a symmetry-constrained PSF; the text still recommends measuring or calibrating (p. 234).

## Safe Wording

Deconvolution requires knowledge of the point spread function; theoretical and measured PSFs can both differ from the real one in a heterogeneous sample, and PSF errors and noise can cause major artifacts, while iterative statistical algorithms converge slowly (Sibarita 2005, Secs. 2.4 and 4, pp. 210-214, 224-230).
