## Why This Matters

A practitioner-oriented review of wide-field 3-D deconvolution that states, in one place, why a PSF is needed, what happens when it is wrong, which algorithm families exist, and where noise amplification, ringing and other artifacts come from. It also treats spherical aberration from refractive-index mismatch as the most common PSF aberration and as something to correct optically first.

## Key Claims (paraphrased)

- **What deconvolution is for (p. 1076, Introduction).** Deconvolution is a computational technique for improving the contrast and resolution of digital images by removing or reversing blur caused by the limited aperture of the objective. [anchor: "technique for improving the contrast"] [anchor: "remove or reverse the blurring present"]
- **Blur versus noise, scatter and glare (pp. 1076-1077, Causes of Image Degradation).** Image degradation is split into noise, scatter, glare and blur; deconvolution mainly removes blur. Blur is non-random and a function of the microscope, so it can be modelled with relative simplicity, whereas scatter arises in the specimen and is difficult to model. [anchor: "sets for itself is to remove blur"] [anchor: "blur is a function"] [anchor: "factory method exists yet to predict"]
- **PSF and convolution (p. 1077, The PSF).** The image is the convolution of the object with the 3-D PSF, so a 2-D image plane contains blurred light from other planes; the best an image can be is an assembly of PSFs. Deconvolution reverses the process to estimate the object. [anchor: "brick is to the house"]
- **PSF may be theoretical or measured (p. 1077; pp. 1088-1090).** The PSF can come from a diffraction model or from a 3-D image of a fluorescent bead. Results are generally better with a measured PSF because theoretical models are imperfect and commercial theoretical PSFs assume perfect axial and rotational symmetry, a misfit that is most serious at high NA (1.2-1.4), where small lens variations matter. [anchor: "software packages generally assumes"] [anchor: "perfect axial and rotational symmetry"]
- **Aberration and the PSF (p. 1078).** An empirical PSF departs from symmetry because of irregularities or misalignment in the light path, mainly the objective. The most common aberration is spherical aberration, an axial asymmetry with a loss of resolution and signal, most often caused by a mismatch between the refractive index of the immersion medium and the mounting medium. Both confocal and deconvolution microscopy depend on the PSF being close to ideal. Deconvolution can partially restore lost resolution but no image processing restores lost signal. - **Deblurring (2-D) methods (pp. 1078-1080).** Nearest-neighbour, multi-neighbour and similar plane-by-plane methods are computationally economical but add noise from several planes, remove blurred signal, can sharpen features in the wrong plane, and improve contrast at the expense of signal-to-noise ratio; quantitative measurements should not be made after them. [anchor: "deblurring algorithms improve contrast,"] [anchor: "should never be performed after"]
- **Inverse filters and noise amplification (p. 1080).** Inverse filters (Wiener, regularized least squares, Tikhonov-Miller) divide the image transform by the PSF transform; the calculation is fast, but division in Fourier space amplifies small noise variations, so blur removal is traded against noise gain, and ringing can appear. Assuming a smooth object (regularization) reduces both at some risk of losing detail. [anchor: "small noise variations in the Fourier"] [anchor: "is traded against a gain in noise"]
- **Constrained iterative methods (pp. 1080-1082).** An estimate is blurred with the PSF, compared with the raw image, and corrected iteratively, usually with nonnegativity and smoothing constraints; the algorithm tends to amplify noise as iterations proceed, so implementations suppress it with smoothing. Classical (Jansson-Van Cittert, with Agard's modification), statistical (maximum likelihood, expectation maximization) and blind variants exist. [anchor: "algorithm will tend to amplify noise, so"] [anchor: "Agard for application to digital micro"]
- **Statistical methods cost more (p. 1082).** Statistical algorithms are more computationally intensive than the classical ones, can take significantly longer, and may restore slightly higher resolution. [anchor: "putationally intensive than the classical"] [anchor: "methods and can take significantly"]
- **Blind deconvolution and aberration (p. 1082).** Blind deconvolution estimates the PSF together with the object, starting from a theoretical PSF, and so can partially correct spherical aberration, but the authors call this a last resort and prefer minimizing spherical aberration at acquisition. ## Relevance to the thesis

- Supports: G25 well in parts. A PSF model is needed (pp. 1077, 1088); a wrong, noisy or aberration-mismatched PSF produces artifacts and loss of quality (p. 1088); inverse filters and iterative methods amplify noise (pp. 1080, 1082, 1092); ringing and other artifacts occur (pp. 1091-1094); iterative methods need many iterations, more so for statistical ones, and substantial memory (pp. 1082-1086). G24 in part: the most common PSF aberration is spherical aberration from refractive-index mismatch, which grows with depth in thick specimens, and high-NA lenses show lens-specific aberration (pp. 1078, 1088-1091). G27 in part: the software PSF is assumed spatially invariant (p. 1090) and the one hardware corrector discussed (a water-immersion objective with correction collar) is described as expensive (p. 1091).
- Does not support: that deconvolution is prohibitively slow or costly, since the paper calls restoration "somewhat slower" than deblurring and gives practical run-time advice (pp. 1082, 1094-1095); that deconvolution inherently lowers signal-to-noise ratio, since restoration is stated to preserve total signal and to give better signal-to-noise ratio than deblurring (pp. 1084, 1086); G26 (no wavefront sensing or adaptive optics); G30 beyond the fact that the input is a 3-D optical-section stack; any statement about Fourier light-field microscopy or scattering inversion (scatter is called hard to model, p. 1076).

## Differences to state if cited

- The review concerns 3-D wide-field (and confocal) deconvolution of fluorescent stacks with a PSF estimated or measured per objective; it is not about light-field or Fourier light-field imaging.
- Software features described (commercial packages of 2001, blind deconvolution only in one product, spatially invariant PSF in all packages) describe the state in 2001 and should not be presented as current.
- Noise amplification is attributed mainly to inverse filters and to iterative methods without sufficient regularization; regularization and nonnegativity are the stated remedies.

## Safe Wording

Deconvolution needs a PSF that matches the blur in the raw image; a noisy, aberrated or mismatched PSF produces artifacts, inverse-filter and iterative methods amplify noise unless regularized, and common packages assume a spatially invariant PSF even though refractive-index mismatch makes it vary with depth (Wallace et al. 2001, pp. 1080, 1088, 1090).
