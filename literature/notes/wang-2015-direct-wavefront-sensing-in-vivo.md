## Why This Matters

This is the paper that applies a Shack-Hartmann sensor with a deformable mirror to a two-photon microscope in scattering tissue. It states exactly why direct sensing needs a guide star whose light is not overwhelmed by scattering, and it states a limit beyond which only indirect (image-based) AO remains. It is also the clearest source for how a Shack-Hartmann correction is local to the measurement site.

## Key Claims (paraphrased)

- **Aberration and scattering degrade tissue imaging (p. 2).** In tissue such as mammalian brain, depth and resolution are limited by scattering and aberration. In two-photon imaging the ballistic focus decays exponentially with depth, and aberrations further stop the surviving photons from forming a diffraction-limited focus, so signal, contrast and resolution fall. Longer excitation wavelength reduces scattering but not aberration. [anchor: "scattering and aberration in tissue such as the mammalian brain"] [anchor: "the surviving ballistic photons from forming a diffraction-limited"]
- **Sources of aberration in this system (p. 2, p. 4).** The cranial window introduces aberrations; they pre-corrected these with the objective correction ring so that AO mainly reflected brain tissue. The corrective wavefronts contained coma, astigmatism and spherical aberration attributed to the refractive-index mismatch between brain tissue and water, plus high-order modes from tissue heterogeneity. In the confocal section, the protective layers over the brain (dura, arachnoid, pia) and a slight tilt of the window are named as sources of aberration on excitation and emission. [anchor: "aberrations that can be corrected either by AO"] [anchor: "caused by the refractive index mismatch between brain tissue and"] [anchor: "mater) as well as slight tilt of the cranial window can impose"]
- **What is sensed and how (p. 2).** Direct wavefront sensing with a Shack-Hartmann (SH) sensor on a fluorescent guide star (GS) created inside the specimen by two-photon excitation (TPE). A lenslet array makes an array of GS images on a camera; shifts of these images from the ideal array give the wavefront, which is applied by a wavefront-modulating element, usable in TPE or single-photon confocal mode. The authors call this a simple, fast and photon-efficient method in earlier (transparent-sample, zebrafish) work. [anchor: "In the SH sensor, a lenslet array creates an"] [anchor: "distortion of a fluorescent guide star"]
- **Why a clean guide star is needed in scattering tissue (p. 2).** The method needs a clear image of the GS in each element of the sensor. Scattering in brain reduces the ballistic fluorescence that forms the GS images and also generates a diffuse background that eventually overwhelms the ballistic component and makes accurate aberration measurement impossible. Scattering falls with longer wavelength, so a near-infrared GS is used. [anchor: "a clear image of the GS is needed in"] [anchor: "diffuse background, which eventually overwhelms the ballistic"] [anchor: "exploiting the reduced scattering of near-infrared guide stars"]
- **Guide-star types tried (p. 2, p. 6).** YFP (emission peak about 530 nm), tdTomato (about 580 nm) and injected indocyanine green (ICG, about 810 nm, excited by two-photon at 1,020 nm). Signal-to-background of the SH spots was best for tdTomato at shallow depth and for ICG beyond about 400 um. In the functional experiments the iRFP near-infrared protein replaced ICG (ICG raised spontaneous activity). [anchor: "Reduced scattering at longer wavelength allows direct wavefront"] [anchor: "direct wavefront sensing can be extended"]
- **Sensor and optical path (p. 5, Methods).** The fluorescence from the TPE guide star is descanned by the galvanometer mirrors, separated from the excitation by a dichroic, relayed onto a lenslet array conjugate to the objective rear pupil, and recorded by an EMCCD at the lenslet focal plane. In two-photon imaging mode the deformable mirror (Alpao DM 97-15) is in the excitation path only: the system works open loop, with the DM shaping only the excitation wavefront. The wavefront is reconstructed modally with the first 55 Zernike modes, which the authors say makes sensing more robust against noise and missing spots. [anchor: "from the TPE GS is descanned by Galvos X and Y"] [anchor: "the AO system is operated in an open-loop manner"] [anchor: "strategy is adopted to make wavefront sensing more robust"]
- **Hardware remark (p. 5).** Compared with the earlier system, which used two spatial light modulators for visible and near-infrared beams, a single deformable mirror is used; the authors say this simplifies the system and works over a wider wavelength range. The paper makes no statement about the price of the deformable mirror or the sensor. [anchor: "simplifies the system but also works over a wider wavelength range"]
- **Limit of direct sensing; fallback to indirect AO (p. 6).** Brighter guide stars can offset the falling signal-to-background at depth, but eventually the brightness required becomes impractical because of sample damage, photobleaching, loss of marginal rays and long measurement time. Beyond these limits, correction via indirect wavefront measurement is the only option. [anchor: "Beyond these limits, AO correction via indirect wavefront"]
- **Correction is local in space (p. 3).** A single correction can improve image quality over a field of hundreds of microns in mouse cortex, but the best performance is obtained when the aberration is measured at the imaging location. Wavefronts measured at three sites 200-400 um apart at 400 um depth were similar, yet in all three cases the best signal and contrast came from the locally measured correction. The authors conclude frequent AO corrections are likely needed to image a large field (for example 1 x 1 mm), and point to the correction speed as suiting that. [anchor: "the best performance is still obtained when the aberration"] [anchor: "the highest signal and greatest contrast recovery occurred when"] [anchor: "frequent AO corrections are likely"]
- **Isoplanatic condition for the sensing scan (p. 4).** The guide star was scanned over a small area (under 60 x 60 um) to reduce bleaching and give better spot patterns; the authors say this does not harm AO performance as long as the area is smaller than the isoplanatic patch. [anchor: "the NIR GS was scanned over"] [anchor: "patch of the tissue, scanning the GS does not compromise AO"]
- **Speed (p. 3, p. 4).** Correction takes milliseconds to seconds; with typical expression levels the authors give under 100 ms to several seconds, insensitive to sample movement and fluorescence variations. The per-experiment values are in Supplementary Table 1 (unread). [anchor: "its high correction speed (milliseconds to seconds; Supplementary"] [anchor: "to several seconds, and is insensitive to sample"]
- **Demonstrated benefit (p. 2-4).** With one wavefront measurement 600 um below the pia, dendritic spines were resolved over a 120 x 120 um field and were mostly invisible without AO (signal gain up to 6x); a spine-neck width fell from about 660 nm to about 430 nm at 550 um depth. Calcium transients were detected in many more neurites at 400, 500 and 600 um depth after AO. Imaging was corrected down to 700-760 um below the pia. [anchor: "dendritic spines could be clearly resolved over a large field of view"] [anchor: "afforded by AO, most of these spines were invisible"] [anchor: "nm before AO to B430 nm after AO"]
- **Deconvolution use (p. 5, Fig. 4 caption).** Some confocal panels in Fig. 4 are labelled "Correction ring, AO and deconvolution"; the main text and caption give no detail of that processing, so this paper is not a source on deconvolution. [anchor: "Correction ring, AO and deconvolution"]

## Relevance to the thesis

- Supports: G26 (a Shack-Hartmann sensor on a two-photon-excited fluorescent guide star plus a deformable mirror; and the explicit statement that scattering makes direct sensing fail without a good guide star, with indirect sensing as the only option past the limits). G24 (aberrations from tissue, cranial window and protective layers degrade two-photon and confocal imaging). G27 (best correction is the locally measured one; one measurement serves a field of hundreds of microns; large fields need frequent re-measurement).
- Does not support: any cost or complexity claim about deformable mirrors (no statement); a claim that corrections are spatially invariant (the paper says the opposite for best performance); G25 (nothing on software cost or noise amplification); G30. Also it does not say that direct sensing is hard in microscopy in general (that is Ji 2010); it says it is hard when scattering is strong, and extends it with an NIR guide star.

## Differences to state if cited

- Two-photon scanning microscope in living mouse cortex with a descanned guide star; sensing is of the fluorescence emission, correction is applied to the excitation (open loop).
- The Shack-Hartmann sensor here sits in a sample-scale point-scanning system, not in a camera-based light-field or widefield microscope.
- Direct sensing needs an injected dye or an expressed protein as the guide star (ICG injection, iRFP expressed by a virus, or the labelled structures themselves).
- Wavefront sensing was done after system aberration compensation, so the wavefronts shown are sample-induced only.
