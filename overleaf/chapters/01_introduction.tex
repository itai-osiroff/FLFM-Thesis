\chapter{Introduction}
Non-invasive biological imaging have made leaps in the 20th and 21st centuries in resolution, speed and sensitivity. Some of the most powerful examples include magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), ultrasound imaging, and CT scans. These technologies have become pillars of modern diagnostic medicine, allowing clinicians to see into the human body, even in real-time, without the need for invasive procedures, revolutionizing patient care, and early detection. Aside from medical application, research fields such as neuroscience, biology, and materials science have also benefited from the ability to inspect deep structures and fast processes in-vivo.

Despite being fundamental to modern medicine and science, the above-mentioned approaches suffer from a variety of limitations, or tradeoffs. Starting with one of the oldest examples, CT scans rely on the use of ionizing radiation to create volumetric images of the body. This method is fast and effective, but is harmful to the patients and must be used as sparingly as possible. MRI, on the other hand, uses the magnetic properties of hydrogen atoms to easily create images of soft tissues and even track cellular activity in real-time, but is slow, not portable, and expensive. Ultrasound imaging manages to avoid the use of ionizing radiation, is quite portable and relatively cheap, but its reliance on sound waves makes it limited in resolution and depth of penetration.

Considering the aforementioned obstacles, optics-based methods have also emerged as useful supplementary tools, mostly for ex-vivo and in vitro applications. Scientific research has utilized fluorescence as the basic imaging mode in frameworks such as confocal microscopy, two-photon microscopy, and light-sheet microscopy have enabled researchers to visualize cellular and subcellular structures with high resolution and specificity.
