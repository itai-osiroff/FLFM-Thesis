0The setup in one picture

Say it simply A Fourier light-field microscope is an ordinary microscope with a microlens array (MLA) placed where the objective's back pupil is imaged. Each lenslet sees the specimen through its own small piece of the pupil, so it records its own small image from its own direction (a view). One camera frame therefore holds many views. The whole section asks: how sharp is a view, how many directions do we get, and what does that do to axial resolution and depth of field?

Walk the path (left to right)

  1. Objective (focal length fobj, numerical aperture NAobj) collects the emission; its back pupil has diameter Dpupil.
  2. Tube lens (fTL) forms the NIP with magnification M = fTL/fobj.
  3. Fourier lens (fFL) makes the pupil image of diameter Dproj on the MLA.
  4. MLA (pitch dMLA, focal length fMLA): every lenslet takes one slice of the pupil and makes a real image of the NIP on the camera plane (the MLA focal plane).
  5. Camera relay (f5, f6, magnification Mcam) shrinks that plane onto the sensor.

The four questions this section answers

Standing assumptions (apply to every section below)

  • Paraxial, small-angle optics: NAMLA ≈ dMLA/(2 fMLA); lens magnifications are ratios of focal lengths (ideal relays; implicit in the text).
  • The Abbe limit λ/(2NA) is the optimal, diffraction-limited resolution of each aperture.
  • Geometry (parallax, windows, blur planes) is done with ray optics; diffraction enters only through the Abbe limit and the classical depth-of-field wave term.
  • Resolution and depth of field are quoted at the object plane, so every NIP or camera-plane quantity is projected back through the magnifications (lateral ÷M, axial ÷M²).
  • Criteria (which two emitters count as “resolved”, which lenslet to use, the pixel as circle of confusion) are taken from Guo.

1Spatial–angular (lateral) resolution trade-off

Source: .tex lines 8–44 · headline: Rxy = ρ λ/(2 NAobj)

Say it simply Each lenslet is a tiny camera with a small aperture, so each view is blurry; but the lenslets look from different angles. A bigger lenslet makes a sharper view and leaves fewer lenslets across the pupil, so fewer angles. Every extra lenslet across the pupil costs one factor of the objective's diffraction limit.

Assumptions used here

  • The lenslet is the smallest aperture in the chain, so resolution is bottlenecked by a single lenslet; the optics after the MLA are ignored for resolution.
  • Abbe limit λ/(2NA) is the optimum, applied to one lenslet only. This is justified because each lenslet forms a real, single image (Fig. above).
  • NAMLA ≈ dMLA/(2 fMLA) (small angle).
  • Abbe sine condition: NAobj = sinθ = (Dpupil/2)/fobj, which gives the pupil size.
  • The pupil is projected onto the MLA by the ratio fFL/fTL.
  • Angular sampling = number of lenslets across the projected pupil.

Derivation A — lateral resolution (spatial)

  1. Spot of one lenslet, on its focal plane
    $$R_{xy}^{\mathrm{MLA}}=\frac{\lambda}{2\,\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{MLA}}}$$
    Abbe limit with the lenslet's own NA.
  2. Project back to the object plane
    A lenslet images the NIP onto its focal plane with magnification $f_{\mathrm{MLA}}/f_{\mathrm{FL}}$, and the objective system adds $M$. Undo both:
    $$R_{xy}=\frac{\lambda}{2\,\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{MLA}}}\times\frac{f_{\mathrm{FL}}}{f_{\mathrm{MLA}}}\times\frac{1}{M}\qquad\text{with}\quad \mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{MLA}}\approx\frac{d_{\mathrm{MLA}}}{2f_{\mathrm{MLA}}}$$
  3. Simplified form mine
    $$R_{xy}=\frac{\lambda\,f_{\mathrm{FL}}}{M\,d_{\mathrm{MLA}}}$$
    Substitute $\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{MLA}}$; $f_{\mathrm{MLA}}$ cancels. Resolution gets finer as the pitch grows.

Derivation B — angular resolution (sampling of the pupil)

  1. Back-pupil size from the Abbe sine condition
    $$\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}}=\sin\theta=\frac{D_{\mathrm{pupil}}/2}{f_{\mathrm{obj}}}\;\Rightarrow\;D_{\mathrm{pupil}}=2\,\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}}\,f_{\mathrm{obj}}$$
  2. Project it onto the MLA
    $$D_{\mathrm{proj}}=2\,\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}}\,f_{\mathrm{obj}}\,\frac{f_{\mathrm{FL}}}{f_{\mathrm{TL}}}=\frac{2\,\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}}\,f_{\mathrm{FL}}}{M}$$
    $f_{\mathrm{TL}}$ is the lens after the objective, $f_{\mathrm{FL}}$ the one before the MLA; $M=f_{\mathrm{TL}}/f_{\mathrm{obj}}$.
  3. Count lenslets across it: the pupil-to-pitch ratio $\rho$
    $$R_{\mathrm{uv}}\equiv\rho=\frac{D_{\mathrm{proj}}}{d_{\mathrm{MLA}}}=\frac{\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}}}{\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{MLA}}}\times\frac{f_{\mathrm{FL}}}{f_{\mathrm{MLA}}}\times\frac{1}{M}$$

Combine A and B

$$R_{xy}=\rho\cdot\frac{\lambda}{2\,\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}}}\qquad\text{since}\quad \rho\,\frac{\lambda}{2\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}}}=\frac{2\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}}f_{\mathrm{FL}}}{M d_{\mathrm{MLA}}}\cdot\frac{\lambda}{2\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}}}=\frac{\lambda f_{\mathrm{FL}}}{M d_{\mathrm{MLA}}}$$
The ratio of Eq. $R_{xy}$ to the wide-field limit $\lambda/(2\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}})$ is exactly $\rho$. At $\rho=1$ (one lenslet, no angular information) we recover wide-field resolution.

Conclusion

  • Direct trade-off, everything else equal: a larger lenslet raises $\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{MLA}}$, so $R_{xy}$ gets finer, but $\rho$ falls because $d_{\mathrm{MLA}}$ is in the denominator. A smaller lenslet does the opposite.
  • Both $R_{xy}$ and $\rho$ scale as $1/d_{\mathrm{MLA}}$, so $R_{xy}/\rho=\lambda/(2\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}})$ is a constant of the objective.

If a student asks

“Why can we ignore the camera relay when computing resolution?” The relay only rescales the image of the MLA focal plane. The smallest aperture in the chain is one lenslet, and its spot is what limits sharpness. The relay's influence enters separately through the pixel size (§4).
“Why is the lenslet, not the objective, the limit?” A view only receives a $1/\rho$ slice of the pupil, so its effective numerical aperture is $\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}}/\rho$. This is the same statement as $R_{xy}=\rho\lambda/(2\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}})$ and returns in the depth-of-field section.

2Axial resolution

Source: .tex lines 46–69 · headline: Rz = Rxy fFL/(dmax M)

Say it simply Depth comes from parallax. The central lenslet sees an in-focus and a defocused point on the optical axis at the same spot. An outer lenslet looks at them at an angle, so the defocused one appears shifted sideways. When that shift equals the lateral blur of a view, the two points are just resolved.

Assumptions used here

  • Classical wide-field axial resolution is $R_{z,\mathrm{class}}\propto\lambda n/\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}}^2$; the FLFM does not follow it (Guo).
  • Test case: two emitters on the optical axis, one in the focal plane and one at a defocus.
  • They are separated in the most extreme non-central sub-image (Guo takes the first diagonal from the centre).
  • “Resolved” means their separation in that sub-image equals the lateral resolution found in §1 (taken as the FWHM).
  • Ray optics for the shift; axial magnification of the objective system is $M^2$.

Derivation

  1. Lenslet resolution as seen at the NIP
    $$R_{\mathrm{NIP}}=\frac{\lambda}{2\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{MLA}}}\times\frac{f_{\mathrm{FL}}}{f_{\mathrm{MLA}}}\qquad(=M\,R_{xy})$$
    Same as $R_{xy}$ before the last $1/M$.
  2. Parallax in the outermost view
    That lenslet's centre is $d_{\mathrm{max}}$ off axis, so it views the emitters at angle $\tan\theta'=d_{\mathrm{max}}/f_{\mathrm{FL}}$. The defocused emitter appears displaced from the focused one by
    $$s=\Delta z_{\mathrm{NIP}}\tan\theta'=\Delta z_{\mathrm{NIP}}\,\frac{d_{\mathrm{max}}}{f_{\mathrm{FL}}}$$
  3. Resolution criterion: set the shift equal to the blur
    $$R_{\mathrm{NIP}}=\Delta z_{\mathrm{NIP}}\cdot\frac{d_{\mathrm{max}}}{f_{\mathrm{FL}}}\;\Rightarrow\;\Delta z_{\mathrm{NIP}}=R_{\mathrm{NIP}}\,\frac{f_{\mathrm{FL}}}{d_{\mathrm{max}}}$$
  4. Project back through the axial magnification $M^2$
    $$R_z=\frac{\Delta z_{\mathrm{NIP}}}{M^2}=R_{xy}\,\frac{f_{\mathrm{FL}}}{d_{\mathrm{max}}\,M}$$
    Uses $R_{\mathrm{NIP}}=M R_{xy}$. Written with the simplified $R_{xy}$ mine: $R_z=\dfrac{\lambda f_{\mathrm{FL}}^2}{M^2\,d_{\mathrm{MLA}}\,d_{\mathrm{max}}}$.

Conclusion

  • Axial resolution is tied to the lateral one ($R_z\propto R_{xy}$) and improves with a longer baseline: the farther the extreme lenslet ($d_{\mathrm{max}}$), the more parallax per unit depth.
  • Any degradation of $R_{xy}$ passes straight into $R_z$ (this returns in §4: pixel sampling worsens both by the same factor).
  • Important note: in Guo's simulation this resolution gets worse with distance from the focal plane. The formula describes emitters near the focal plane.

If a student asks

“Why $M^2$?” The axial magnification of an imaging system is the square of its lateral magnification, so depth at the NIP is $M^2$ times depth in the object (ΔzNIP = M²Δz).
“Why the central lenslet as reference?” It looks straight along the axis, so both on-axis emitters coincide there. Any off-axis lenslet adds the parallax; the outermost gives the most.

3Depth of field

Source: .tex lines 71–99 · headline: DOF = the smaller of a cross-talk limit and a diffraction-plus-pixel blur limit

Say it simply Two things can end the usable depth range. First, an emitter that moves off the focal plane shifts sideways in each off-axis view; if it shifts out of that view's window it leaks into the neighbouring view (cross-talk). Second, one view has a small effective aperture, so it blurs quickly with defocus. The depth of field is whichever limit is shorter.

Assumptions used here

  • Guo's two considerations: diffraction (wave optics plus circle of confusion) and avoiding microlens cross-talk.
  • Cross-talk: each view records only a window of the NIP of width FOVNIP = dMLA fFL/fMLA; an in-focus emitter sits at the window centre; the shift is evaluated for the nearest lenslet, $\tan\theta_1=d_{\mathrm{MLA}}/f_{\mathrm{FL}}$ (following Guo).
  • Blur: a view collects a slice of the pupil of width $D_{\mathrm{proj}}/\rho$, so its effective NA is $\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}}/\rho$; the classical formula $\frac{\lambda n}{\mathrm{NA}^2}+\frac{n\,c}{M\,\mathrm{NA}}$ is adapted to one view with $n=1$, circle of confusion $c=P_{\mathrm{eff}}$, $M_{\mathrm{eff}}=M f_{\mathrm{MLA}}/f_{\mathrm{FL}}$, $\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}}/\rho$.
  • The camera pixel (projected, $P_{\mathrm{eff}}=P/M_{\mathrm{cam}}$) is the maximum blur allowed.

3a — Cross-talk limit (geometry only)

  1. Shift allowed before the light enters the neighbouring window
    In focus the emitter is at the window centre, so it may move by $\mathrm{FOV}_{\mathrm{NIP}}/2$ either way. The shift per unit defocus is $\tan\theta_1$.
  2. Allowed axial range at the NIP = window width ÷ shift per unit defocus
    $$\mathrm{DOF}_{\mathrm{NIP}}=\frac{\mathrm{FOV}_{\mathrm{NIP}}}{\tan\theta_1}=d_{\mathrm{MLA}}\frac{f_{\mathrm{FL}}}{f_{\mathrm{MLA}}}\times\frac{f_{\mathrm{FL}}}{d_{\mathrm{MLA}}}=\frac{f_{\mathrm{FL}}^2}{f_{\mathrm{MLA}}}$$
  3. Project to object space
    $$\mathrm{DOF}_{\mathrm{proj}}=\mathrm{DOF}_{\mathrm{NIP}}\times\frac{1}{M^2}$$
Purely geometric: the pitch cancels, and neither wavelength nor pixel size enters.

3b — Diffraction and pixel-size limit (blur of one view)

  1. Start from the classical formula and plug in the effective single-view system
    $$\mathrm{DOF}=\underbrace{\frac{\lambda\,\rho^2}{\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}}^2}}_{\text{wave term }a}+\underbrace{P_{\mathrm{eff}}\,\frac{\rho}{\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}}}\,\frac{f_{\mathrm{FL}}}{f_{\mathrm{MLA}}}\,\frac{1}{M}}_{\text{pixel term }p},\qquad P_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{P}{M_{\mathrm{cam}}}$$
    Wave: $\lambda/\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{eff}}^2=\lambda\rho^2/\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}}^2$. Pixel: $c/(M_{\mathrm{eff}}\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{eff}})$ with the substitutions above.
  2. Eliminate $\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}}$ with $\rho=2\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}}f_{\mathrm{FL}}/(M d_{\mathrm{MLA}})$
    $$a=\frac{4\lambda f_{\mathrm{FL}}^2}{M^2d_{\mathrm{MLA}}^2},\qquad p=\frac{2P_{\mathrm{eff}}f_{\mathrm{FL}}^2}{M^2d_{\mathrm{MLA}}f_{\mathrm{MLA}}}$$ $$\Rightarrow\; \mathrm{DOF}=\frac{2f_{\mathrm{FL}}^2}{M^2d_{\mathrm{MLA}}}\left(\frac{2\lambda}{d_{\mathrm{MLA}}}+\frac{P_{\mathrm{eff}}}{f_{\mathrm{MLA}}}\right)$$
$P$ is the camera pixel pitch and $M_{\mathrm{cam}}$ the magnification of the relay between the MLA focal plane and the camera.

Conclusion

  • The depth of field is the smaller of $f_{\mathrm{FL}}^2/(M^2 f_{\mathrm{MLA}})$ and the blur expression. In Guo, and for our parameters, the cross-talk limit is not the limiting factor; the blur of a single view is.
  • Important note: the objective NA cancels out of the final blur DOF. Both terms scale as $f_{\mathrm{FL}}^2/M^2$; the wave term as $1/d_{\mathrm{MLA}}^2$, the pixel term as $P_{\mathrm{eff}}/(d_{\mathrm{MLA}}f_{\mathrm{MLA}})$. For fixed focal lengths and relay, a larger lenslet shortens the depth of field (while sharpening $R_{xy}$).

If a student asks

“Why is the pixel a depth-of-field term?” It is the circle of confusion: the largest blur we tolerate is one projected pixel, so the defocus that blurs a point to a pixel sets the pixel term.
“Why does NAobj cancel?” A bigger NA makes the pupil larger, which increases $\rho$ and thins each slice by the same factor, so the effective aperture of one view does not change with $\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}}$ when the lenslet is fixed.

4Magnifications, camera spec and MLA spec

Source: .tex lines 101–133 · two checks: does the pupil fit the sensor, and is the pixel small enough

Say it simply The camera must see the whole pupil image (sensor size), and its pixels must be small enough to sample the sharpness a view can deliver (pixel size). Our pupil image is bigger than the sensor, so a demagnifying relay shrinks it. Our pixels, projected back to the object, are a bit too coarse, and that costs lateral resolution, axial resolution, and a little depth of field.

Assumptions used here

  • The sensor should contain the full angular field, i.e. the pupil image of diameter $D_{\mathrm{proj}}$ (after the camera relay).
  • Nyquist criterion (following Guo): two resolved point sources need at least one pixel between them.
  • The projected pixel is $p=P/M_T$ with total magnification $M_T=M\cdot\dfrac{f_{\mathrm{MLA}}}{f_{\mathrm{FL}}}\cdot M_{\mathrm{cam}}$.

4a — Sensor size: does the pupil image fit?

$$D_{\mathrm{cam}}\;\ge\;M_{\mathrm{cam}}\,D_{\mathrm{proj}}=M_{\mathrm{cam}}\,\frac{2\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}}}{M}\,f_{\mathrm{FL}}$$
$D_{\mathrm{proj}}$ is the magnified back pupil of the objective. The relay (here $f_5=400$ mm, $f_6=150$ mm, so $M_{\mathrm{cam}}=0.375$) shrinks it onto the sensor.

Worked numbers

4b — Pixel size: three effects

  1. One pixel in object space
    $$p=\frac{P}{M_T}\approx 2\,\mu\text{m}$$
  2. Sampling of the lateral resolution
    $$S_r=\frac{R_{xy}}{P/M_T}=\frac{\lambda}{2\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{MLA}}}\times\frac{1}{P_{\mathrm{eff}}}=\frac{\lambda}{2\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{MLA}}}\times\frac{M_{\mathrm{cam}}}{P}$$
    Number of pixels spanned by the resolution limit. Nyquist wants about 2. Ours is about 1.33, i.e. undersampled.
  3. Consequences of coarse pixels
    1. Lateral: the bound is what two pixels can separate, so instead of 2.65 µm we get $2p\approx4\,\mu$m.
    2. Axial: it relies on the same $R_{xy}$, so it worsens by the same factor, about $\times1.5$.
    3. Depth of field: only weakly dependent. Using $R_{xy}=\rho\lambda/(2\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}})\Rightarrow\rho/\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}}=2R_{xy}/\lambda$ and $p=P_{\mathrm{eff}}\frac{f_{\mathrm{FL}}}{f_{\mathrm{MLA}}}\frac1M$: $$\mathrm{DOF}=\frac{\lambda\rho^2}{\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}}^2}+p\,\frac{\rho}{\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}}}=\frac{2R_{xy}^2}{\lambda}\left(2+\frac{1}{S_r}\right)$$

Conclusion

  • The relay is needed because $D_{\mathrm{proj}}$ exceeds the sensor, and the chosen demagnification satisfies the fit with room to spare.
  • Pixel size, not optics, ends up limiting lateral and axial resolution (about $\times1.5$ worse than the optical values), while depth of field is barely affected.

Take-away slide (five lines)

  1. Views are blurrier than wide-field by exactly the number of lenslets across the pupil: $R_{xy}=\rho\,\lambda/(2\mathrm{NA}_{\mathrm{obj}})$.
  2. So lenslet size trades sharpness against angular samples, and both scale as $1/d_{\mathrm{MLA}}$.
  3. Axial resolution is parallax: $R_z=R_{xy}f_{\mathrm{FL}}/(d_{\mathrm{max}}M)$, so it follows $R_{xy}$ and the baseline.
  4. Depth of field is the shorter of cross-talk ($f_{\mathrm{FL}}^2/(M^2f_{\mathrm{MLA}})$) and single-view blur; blur wins, and bigger lenslets shorten it.
  5. The camera must hold the pupil (relay $\times0.375$) and sample it: $S_r\approx1.33$ means pixel-limited lateral and axial resolution (about $\times1.5$ worse), with only a weak DOF cost.
