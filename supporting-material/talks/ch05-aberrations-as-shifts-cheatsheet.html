Aberrations as shifts

In an FLFM, an aberration mostly moves each view instead of blurring it. Each view moves differently. Measure the moves, undo them, and average.

Talk order below. Each step has the sentence to say, the picture, and the one equation. Step 4 is interactive. Stay in the geometry of Figure 3.1 until step 3; say “slope” only after the picture is set.

1

Start from what she already accepts: each view is a small microscope

Say

“Each lenslet passes one patch of the pupil, so each view is a small-aperture microscope looking at the same flat scene through its own patch. A ray's direction in object space and the point where it crosses the pupil are the same thing.”

Pupil coordinate ↔ ray angle: a ray leaving the specimen at angle \(\theta\) crosses the back pupil at \(u = f_{obj}\sin\theta\) (sine condition). Patch \(k\) therefore collects one band of angles, of width \(2NA_{view}\) in \(\sin\theta\), centred on \(\sin\theta_k = 2k\,NA_{view}\), \(k=-\tfrac{N-1}{2},\dots,\tfrac{N-1}{2}\).

2

An aberration is a path error over the pupil; over one small patch it only tilts the rays

Say

“An aberration adds a different optical path at each point of the pupil; call it \(W(u,v)\). Rays leave perpendicular to the wavefront, so where \(W\) changes across a patch, the rays through that patch are deflected, and the whole view lands sideways. A patch is small, so across it \(W\) is almost a plane: the view moves, it barely blurs.”

The one equation

\[ \mathbf d_k \;=\; \Big\langle \nabla_{s} W \Big\rangle_{\text{patch }k} \;=\; f_{obj}\,\Big\langle \nabla_{u,v} W \Big\rangle_{\text{patch }k} \]

\(\mathbf d_k\): shift of view \(k\) in object space. \(s=\sin\theta\) (direction sine), \(W\) in length units. Sign depends on convention; say “moves by the mean slope”.

Why it is a shift (if asked)

A pupil phase that is linear in \(s\), \(W=a\,s\), is a linear phase on the angular spectrum. By the Fourier shift theorem it translates the image by exactly \(a\). Over one patch, \(W\approx W(s_k)+\nabla W\cdot(s-s_k)\): the linear term shifts the view; what is left (curvature over the patch) blurs it slightly. That remainder is the “to first order”.

The centroid of a spot is exactly the mean gradient over its aperture, so “mean slope over the patch” is not an approximation for the centroid.

3

Three cases, from easy to general

Say

“Tilt moves every view equally, which is harmless. Defocus moves each view in proportion to its position in the pupil, which is the parallax of Chapter 3, and undoing it is refocusing. A general aberration gives each view its own shift; the lenslet array is then a Shack–Hartmann sensor, with the scene in place of a guide star.”

4

The punchline: why the conventional image blurs and the FLFM can correct it

Say

“A conventional microscope adds all the patches on one sensor, so the shifted copies pile up into a blur and the shifts are lost. The FLFM records each patch as its own image, so we can see each shift, undo it, and then average. That is the whole reconstruction; the next section only says how the shifts are found.”

5

What it assumes and what it does not fix

Say

“Three limits. What is left inside one patch still blurs that view. The scene has to be flat, or its depth adds its own parallax. And if the aberration changes across the field, one shift per view is not enough, which is why there is a second, local stage.”

Residual inside a patch

Only the mean slope over a patch becomes a shift. The curvature of \(W\) across one lenslet blurs the view and is not corrected. It is small because a patch is \(1/N\) of the pupil; it is the same reason each view has a large depth of field.

Flat scene

\(\mathrm{Range}_{z,LF}\) only says each view is sharp. A point at depth \(z\) still shifts by \(z\tan\theta_k\). One shift per view needs all emitters at one depth, to within \(\Delta z_{LF}\). The draft's “holds within \(\mathrm{Range}_{z,LF}\)” is too weak.

Field-varying aberration

Scattering layers and a non-flat medium change \(W\) from one field point to the next, so the shift varies across a view. Global stage: one shift per view. Local stage: a shift per pixel. This is the bridge to 5.2.

6

Questions she may ask

QuestionShort answer
Do you estimate \(W\)?No. The method measures the shifts directly by correlation. \(W\) is only the explanation of why shifts are the right thing to measure.
Why the central view as reference?Shifts are only measurable relative to some view. A shift common to all views (tilt) is invisible and harmless; the central view sets zero.
Isn't defocus just the parallax of Chapter 3?Yes, the same effect: \(\partial(\Delta z\cos\theta)/\partial\sin\theta=-\Delta z\tan\theta\). Chapter 3 uses it to resolve depth; here it is one case of a shift to undo.
What do you pay?Each view resolves \(\Delta x_{LF}=N\Delta x_o\). The corrected image is sharp at the view resolution, not at the objective's. More views sample \(W\) more finely but make every view coarser.
Why average instead of taking the best view?After alignment, averaging \(K^2\) views raises signal to noise, and each view looks through a different patch, so detail lost in one is kept in others.
How large can a shift be?Up to the search bound of the global stage. Content must still lie inside the view's field after the shift.
Why is the shift exact for the centroid?The centroid of a spot equals the mean of \(\nabla W\) over the aperture (a standard wavefront-sensing result); cross-correlation peaks near it when the residual blur is small.

Sentence skeleton for 5.1 (≤ 270 words), to show her before writing it in

  • P1, the picture (≈80 words). A nominally flat scene, all emitters within \(\Delta z_{LF}\) of one plane. Each view is a small microscope through one patch (Fig. 3.1); incoherent emitters, so each view is the sum of their images; without aberration the views agree up to a shift. The opening paragraph of the chapter keeps no second statement of flatness.
  • P2, aberration becomes a shift (≈70 words). Define \(W(u,v)\), the optical path error over the pupil, in one clause. Rays leave perpendicular to the wavefront, so over one patch the aberration deflects them and the view moves by the mean gradient of \(W\) over that patch; the remaining curvature blurs the view slightly.
  • P3, the three cases (≈80 words). Tilt: equal shifts. Defocus: shift \(\Delta z\tan\theta_k\), linear in the view's position, the parallax of \cref{sec:factors-of-n}; undoing it is refocusing \cite{levoy2009…}. General: each view its own shift; the array is a Shack–Hartmann sensor with the scene as guide star \cite{platt2001…}; defocus is the quadratic case \cite{wu2021…}.
  • P4, why this corrects (≈40 words). A conventional microscope sums these shifted copies into a blur; the FLFM records them apart, so shifting each view back and averaging removes the aberration, to the view's resolution.
